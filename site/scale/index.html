Finding information takes seconds. Knowing whether to act on it takes training.

The SCALE Pilot (Scientific Communication + AI Literacy Experience) teaches Central Connecticut State University students to evaluate evidence across disciplines, test AI output against primary sources, and explain what they find to the people who make decisions. In summer 2027, students apply those skills to real projects in paid micro-internships with Connecticut organizations.

The gap employers describe

Early-career hires can pull up a study, a vendor report, or an AI summary in seconds. Many cannot yet judge whether the evidence behind it is strong enough to act on, or explain that judgment to the person who has to decide.

SCALE teaches that step. We call it translational literacy: evaluating, verifying, and communicating complex evidence accurately across fields, tools, and audiences. Students learn to use AI as a decision-support tool they question, not a substitute for judgment.

Cross-disciplinary evidence evaluation

Judge a claim by the evidence standards of the field that produced it, and separate warranted findings from overclaims.

AI interrogation

Use generative AI to find leads, check what it says against primary sources, document where it fails, and leave a verification trail anyone can follow.

Expert-to-public boundary crossing

Turn complex evidence into plain language for non-specialists without losing the meaning or the uncertainty.

Metacognitive calibration and integration

Match confidence to the evidence and deliver a clear recommendation: what we know, what we don't know, and what to verify next.

The course: Translational Literacy in Science

Every field decides what counts as evidence in its own way. A biologist, an economist, and an anthropologist can study the same question and hold their findings to different standards. The course makes those standards explicit, so students can judge a claim by the rules of the field it came from and then explain it to someone outside that field.

How the semester runs

  1. Weeks 1–2

    Foundations, information literacy, and the AI interrogation protocol students use all semester.

  2. Weeks 3–12

    Ten discipline modules, alternating natural and social science in deliberate pairs. Biology meets psychology: both run experiments, one on organisms and one on people. Physics meets political science: both build predictive models, one with mathematical certainty and one with probabilistic inference.

  3. Weeks 13–15

    The final project, pitches to a panel, and reflection.

The final project: a cross-domain evidence pitch

Each student teaches themselves two discipline modules not covered in class, builds a realistic problem for a Connecticut employer that needs both, runs the AI interrogation protocol, writes a one-page brief for a non-specialist decision-maker, and delivers a seven-minute pitch. The three instructors, faculty from Communication and English, and partner representatives hear the pitches. Five criteria carry equal weight: module comprehension, synthesis, AI interrogation, calibration, and translation.

The course is the dress rehearsal. The micro-internship is the performance.

What the work looks like

Any organization that explains evidence to people who are not experts can host. The host keeps every deliverable.

Libraries and cultural organizations

Turn program outcome data into a report the community can read. Evaluate a digital platform vendor's claims. Produce patron-facing materials. Examine how outreach reaches underserved communities.

Nonprofits

Check an AI-drafted grant narrative against its sources. Weigh a vendor's pitch for donor software. Summarize a funder's guidelines for the board.

Manufacturers

Evaluate a safety vendor's injury-reduction claim. Turn a revised safety data sheet into a one-page notice for the floor with the caveats intact. Trace an AI-drafted handbook update back to statute and agency guidance.

Finance, technology, and health

Translate regulatory changes for clients. Check AI-generated recommendations before they reach a client. Explain health evidence to the public without distorting it.

Questions hosts ask

Do we need AI expertise?
No. Students bring the method. You bring the problem.
Will the student see confidential material?
Only what you choose to share. Projects are designed to work from documents you select, and they do not require personnel records or proprietary data.
What won't the student do?
Select vendors, set policy, make regulatory determinations, or release anything outside your organization without your review. Students recommend; your staff decide.
How many students can we host?
One is fine, and a site can take more than one. We need 20 positions in total, so hosts that can support several interns help most.
Can we keep working with a strong student?
Talk with us. The credit-bearing project runs 20 to 25 hours, and it gives you a close look at a student you might want to bring back.
What if the match is a barrier?
Tell us. For small nonprofits, match support may be available.

After the pilot

A certificate pathway

The course is the first piece of a proposed Certificate in Applied Scientific Communication (11–12 credits), open to students in any major, including transfer students and returning adults. It pairs Translational Literacy in Science with a Communication course (COMM 115, 215, 334, or 356), Technical Writing (WRT 403), College to Career Transition (PSY/CLSS 413), and the micro-internship.

The proposal moves through CCSU governance after the pilot, informed by student outcomes and host feedback.

More students, more hosts

Plans for 2027–28 call for two sections of the course and more placements as the host network grows.

Students not placed in summer 2027 stay eligible for later placements through the Hub and CCSU's Community Engagement Experiential Learning Stipend.

Materials others can use

The fifteen modules will be released under a CC BY-NC 4.0 license. The modular design lets other disciplines and campuses, including across the Connecticut State Colleges & Universities system, adopt the course without redesign.
