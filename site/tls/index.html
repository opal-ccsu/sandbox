The course

Every field has its own rules for evidence

A biologist, an economist, and an anthropologist can study the same question and hold their findings to different standards. This course makes those standards explicit. You learn to judge a claim by the rules of the field it came from, and then explain it to someone outside that field.

You will use generative AI all semester, as a tool you question. It is good at finding leads. It is also confidently wrong. You learn to tell which is which, and to leave a trail anyone can check.

Here is the kind of work you will be able to do by May.

An AI-drafted summary for a board meeting says a vendor’s reading app “improves literacy outcomes by 30%.”

Traced to the source: the vendor’s own pilot. One school, 48 students, no comparison group. The 30% is a gain on the app’s built-in quiz, not on an independent reading test.

What we know
Students who used the app scored higher on the app’s own quiz.
What we don’t know
Whether the app caused the gain, or whether reading improved anywhere outside the app.
What to verify next
Results from a study with a comparison group and an independent reading measure.

Illustrative example, condensed from the one-page brief students write for a decision-maker.

What you leave with

Translational literacy, plus skills you can name in an interview

Translational literacy is the ability to evaluate, verify, and communicate complex evidence accurately across fields, tools, and audiences. The course builds it in four parts.

Cross-disciplinary evidence evaluation

Judge a claim by the evidence standards of the field that produced it. Separate a warranted finding from an overclaim.

AI interrogation

Use generative AI to find leads, check what it says against primary sources, document where it fails, and leave a verification trail.

Expert-to-public translation

Turn complex evidence into plain language for non-specialists without losing the meaning or the uncertainty.

Calibration

Match your confidence to the evidence and deliver a clear recommendation: what we know, what we don’t, and what to verify next.

The career-specific knowledge, skills, and abilities you build

On the Career Readiness Pathway these are your CS KSAs: the specific things you can point to when an employer asks what you learned. This course is built to add to them, whatever your major.

KnowledgeHow ten fields make and test claims

  • Experiments, two ways. Biology and psychology both run experiments, one on organisms and one on people. You learn what each one can and cannot conclude.
  • Models, two ways. Physics predicts with mathematical certainty. Political science predicts with probability. You learn what a forecast from each one is worth.
  • Information literacy. Where evidence comes from, and how to find the primary source behind a claim.

SkillsWork products you will have made

  • A verification trail. You run the same AI interrogation protocol all semester, and you can show your steps.
  • A one-page brief for a non-specialist decision-maker, built on a realistic problem for a Connecticut employer.
  • A seven-minute pitch delivered to the instructors, faculty from Communication and English, and partner organizations.

AbilitiesWhat carries into any job

  • Teaching yourself a new field. For the final project you learn two discipline modules on your own, then use both on one problem.
  • Saying how sure you are. Every brief ends with what is known, what is not, and what to check next.

On the Career Readiness Pathway, the course builds your CS KSAs and core competencies. The summer micro-internship adds the other two components: a real experiential learning opportunity and a mentor who watches your work.

How the semester runs

Fifteen weeks, three stages

  1. Weeks 1–2. Foundations, information literacy, and the AI interrogation protocol you will use all semester.
  2. Weeks 3–12. Ten discipline modules, alternating natural and social science in deliberate pairs, so you see two fields handle the same kind of question differently.
  3. Weeks 13–15. The final project: a cross-domain evidence pitch. You teach yourself two modules not covered in class, build a realistic problem for a Connecticut employer that needs both, run the AI interrogation protocol, write a one-page brief, and pitch it to a panel. Five criteria carry equal weight: module comprehension, synthesis, AI interrogation, calibration, and translation.

Questions

Questions students ask

Do I have to do the internship?
No. The course stands on its own. The internship is an opportunity for up to 20 students, chosen on their final-project performance.
Will I use AI in this course?
Yes, every week, and on purpose. You learn to use it to find leads and then check everything it tells you against the sources.
Is the internship in person?
Each placement is hybrid or virtual, arranged with the host.
How are interns paid?
The Central Workforce and Innovation Hub handles onboarding, timesheets, and payroll. The $1,000 is split between the grant and your host organization.
